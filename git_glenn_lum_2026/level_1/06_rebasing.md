# L1-06 · Rebasing

Most engineers learn rebasing as a second merge — a different button that achieves roughly the same outcome with a tidier-looking log. That framing is the source of nearly every rebase disaster you'll ever witness or cause. Merge and rebase answer fundamentally different questions, and the moment you treat them as interchangeable is the moment you're one keystroke away from destroying a colleague's work. The question merge answers is "how do I combine these two histories into one?" The question rebase answers is "how do I pretend I started from somewhere else?" Those are not the same question, and the mechanism that makes rebase work is also the mechanism that makes it dangerous.

The mental model worth holding is that rebase doesn't move commits. It replays them. Git walks each commit on your branch, extracts the diff that commit represents, and applies that diff on top of a new base — one at a time, in order. Each replayed commit gets a brand-new SHA, because (and you already know this from the object model) a commit's identity is the hash of its content plus its parent pointer, and you just changed its parent pointer. The original commits still exist in the object database, unreferenced, waiting for garbage collection. What looks like "moving my branch to a newer base" is actually "constructing a parallel sequence of new commits that happen to contain the same diffs." This distinction sounds pedantic until it's the thing that explains every confusing rebase outcome you'll ever see.

Because rebase replays commits one at a time, it hits conflicts the same way merge does — three-way diffs against a common ancestor — but it surfaces them in a different shape. A merge presents you with one consolidated conflict containing every overlapping change. A rebase walks you through conflicts commit by commit, pausing at each one, letting you resolve just that commit's contribution before continuing. This is sometimes genuinely better (each conflict is smaller and scoped to a single logical change) and sometimes genuinely worse (you can hit a "ghost" conflict in commit three that wouldn't have appeared in a merge, because you're resolving against intermediate states the merge would have skipped over). Neither tool is universally easier; they're just shaped differently.

The reason rebase exists at all is that the DAG you produce with merges is an accurate but messy record of how work actually happened — every divergence and reconvergence preserved, every "merge main into my branch" detour visible forever. Rebase trades that literal accuracy for a constructed narrative: a linear sequence of commits that looks as if you wrote them, in order, on top of the latest main, even though you didn't. Whether that trade is worth making is a values question, not a technical one, and it's the actual substance underneath every "rebase vs merge" argument you've seen. Linear history is easier to read, bisect, and revert; literal history is easier to trust as a record of what really happened. Reasonable teams disagree.

Now the dangerous part. Because every rebased commit has a new SHA, a rebased branch is, from Git's perspective, a different branch that happens to contain similar-looking changes. If you've already pushed the original branch and a colleague has based work on it (or merely pulled it), their Git now believes in commits that, after your rebase, no longer exist in your version of history. When you force-push the rebased branch — and you will have to force-push, because the new commits aren't descendants of the old ones — you've replaced shared history with an alternate timeline. Your colleague's next pull will either fail loudly or, worse, succeed in a way that quietly entangles the old and new histories. Commits get duplicated. Work gets lost. The branch becomes a forensic puzzle. This is why "never rebase public branches" exists as a rule, and now you can see it isn't superstition — it's a direct consequence of "rebase creates new commits, force-push overwrites the remote."

The corollary is the rule you should actually internalize: rebase is a tool for shaping history you haven't shared yet. Your local feature branch, before you push it for review? Rebase freely. Squash, reorder, split, edit messages, replay onto a fresher main — that history is yours alone, and a clean commit series is a gift to your reviewers. The instant that branch is pushed and others may have pulled it, the calculus inverts. From that moment, prefer merge to bring main's changes in, or coordinate explicitly (and use `--force-with-lease` rather than `--force`, which at least refuses to overwrite if the remote has moved since you last fetched) if you absolutely must rebase.

The skill rebasing actually builds, then, isn't typing `git rebase` correctly — it's developing a sharp instinct for which side of the "shared yet?" line a given branch is on, and choosing your tool accordingly. Get that boundary right, and rebase becomes one of the most useful operations in Git, the thing that lets you present polished work for review instead of a stream-of-consciousness commit log. Get it wrong, and you'll be the person whose name comes up in the post-mortem.

**Rebase as "replay," not "move"** — Covers the precise mechanic of how Git extracts each commit's diff and reapplies it on a new base, producing new SHAs even when the content is byte-identical. Worth a deeper pass because almost every counterintuitive rebase behavior — duplicate commits after a botched pull, "lost" work that's actually still in the object database, the necessity of force-pushing — falls out of internalizing this one mechanic.

**Interactive rebase (`-i`)** — Covers the editor-driven workflow for squashing, reordering, splitting, rewording, and dropping commits in a branch before sharing it. Worth a Level 2 because this is where rebase stops being a synchronization tool and becomes a narrative tool — the way you turn a messy local history into a reviewable commit series — and the todo-list interface has enough operations (pick, squash, fixup, edit, reword, drop) to be worth practicing deliberately.

**`--onto` for surgical base changes** — Covers the variant that lets you replay a specific range of commits onto an arbitrary new base, rather than the default "rebase my whole branch onto its upstream." Worth deeper study because it's the escape hatch when a dependency branch gets merged, abandoned, or rewritten out from under you, and the three-argument form (`--onto newbase upstream branch`) is genuinely confusing until you see it as "take the commits between upstream and branch, replay them onto newbase."

**Rebase vs merge as a team policy decision** — Covers the actual tradeoff — linear constructed history vs preserved literal history — and how it interacts with code review, bisecting, reverting, and release engineering. Worth going deeper because most teams adopt a policy by accident or imitation, and being able to articulate the tradeoff on its merits is what lets you participate in (or settle) the recurring debate instead of holding a position by reflex.

**Why you never rebase a shared/public branch** — Covers the failure mode in concrete detail: what happens to collaborators' clones when shared history is rewritten, how force-push interacts with their local refs, and what `--force-with-lease` actually checks before allowing the push. Worth a deeper pass because the rule is widely repeated but rarely explained mechanically, and understanding the exact failure sequence is what turns "never do this" into "here's when I can do this safely and here's when I categorically cannot."

#### L1-06 · Rebasing
Rebasing answers a different question than merging: instead of "combine these two histories," it asks "replay my changes as if I'd started from a more current point." Mechanically, this means Git takes each of your commits, computes its diff, and reapplies that diff on top of a new base — which is why rebase can hit the *same* conflicts a merge would, but resolves them one commit at a time rather than once in aggregate. This is the topic most likely to cause real damage if misunderstood, because rebasing rewrites commit SHAs (a "new" commit is created even if the content is identical), which is catastrophic on shared/pushed history and the direct cause of the "never rebase public branches" rule you've probably heard without understanding why.
