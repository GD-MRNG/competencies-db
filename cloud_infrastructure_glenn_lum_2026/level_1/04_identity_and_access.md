# L1-04 · Identity & Access

Here is the thing most practitioners get backwards: they treat identity as a gate they pass through once, at login, and then forget about. In reality, identity is not a gate — it is a claim that has to be re-evaluated on every single action, by every actor, human or otherwise, for the entire lifetime of your system. The moment you internalize that difference, the entire sprawl of provider IAM terminology — roles, policies, service accounts, workload identities, trust relationships, assumed roles — stops looking like a pile of unrelated features and starts looking like variations on a single question asked over and over: who is acting right now, and what are they allowed to do?

That question is the precondition for everything else you rent from a cloud provider. Compute, storage, networking — each of them silently assumes you have already answered it. There is no such thing as "store this object" or "run this workload" without an implied "...as someone who is allowed to." This is why identity isn't one domain among ten; it's the floor the other nine stand on. When identity is wrong, nothing else you built matters, because the wrong person or the wrong process can undo all of it. When identity is right, most of the other domains inherit their security posture from it for free.

The mental model worth carrying is that every access decision reduces to three things: a credential (the thing that proves who you are), a scope (the set of actions that credential is permitted to perform), and a lifetime (how long that credential remains valid). Almost every IAM design choice, and almost every IAM failure, is a decision about one of these three. A password is a credential with a scope of "everything this user can do" and, historically, a lifetime of "until someone changes it." An access key handed to a script is a credential whose scope is whatever policy you attached and whose lifetime, if you're careless, is "forever." Once you see access this way, you can interrogate any provider's system by asking a single diagnostic question: what happens when each of these three is compromised, independently?

The lifetime dimension is where the industry learned its hardest lesson, and it's worth dwelling on. A long-lived credential is a breach with no natural expiry. If a static key leaks — committed to a public repo, logged in plaintext, pulled from a compromised laptop — it keeps working until a human notices and revokes it, and humans notice slowly. The entire shift toward short-lived, identity-bound access exists to collapse that window. When a credential is issued fresh for a task and expires in minutes, a leak is a problem for minutes, not months. This is why modern systems prefer a workload that requests a temporary token tied to its identity over a workload that carries a permanent key in its environment variables. The permanent key isn't dangerous the day it leaks; it's dangerous the day it's created, because from that moment it is a liability waiting for an accident.

Scope is where the concept of least-privilege lives, and least-privilege is badly served by being filed under "compliance." It is not a checkbox. It is a blast-radius decision you make in advance of an incident you have not had yet. Every permission you grant is a permission that can be abused — by an attacker who steals the credential, by a bug in the code the credential authorizes, or by a well-meaning operator who runs the wrong command. When you scope a credential tightly, you are pre-deciding how much damage is possible when — not if — that credential is misused. The tension is real and worth naming honestly: tight scope creates operational friction now (more roles to manage, more "permission denied" errors to debug), in exchange for a smaller blast radius later. Teams that resist that friction by granting broad permissions are not saving time; they are borrowing it against an incident, at interest.

It also matters that you stop conflating two kinds of actor. A human logging in and a piece of code calling an API are both identities, but they are different threat models, and treating them the same is a common and expensive mistake. A human identity assumes a person behind it — subject to phishing, capable of multi-factor prompts, expected to log in from somewhere plausible. A machine identity is code acting on its own behalf, with no one to answer a push notification, often running thousands of times an hour, and frequently over-provisioned because nobody wanted to debug its permissions at 2am. The failure modes diverge sharply: humans get phished, machines get their keys exfiltrated and replayed. A system that authenticates both with the same mechanisms and reasons about both with the same assumptions has a gap it can't see.

Two more distinctions round out the model. The first is that authentication and authorization are separate failure points, not one. Authentication answers "are you who you claim to be"; authorization answers "are you allowed to do this specific thing." A request can pass the first and fail the second, and it should — being logged in is not the same as being permitted. Systems that collapse these two ("if you're in, you can do anything") are the ones where a single compromised low-privilege account becomes a full breach. The second is federation: the discipline of not re-creating identity from scratch for every provider and every system you touch. An organization with a working identity source can delegate trust — letting one system vouch for a user or a workload to another — so that offboarding a person or rotating a trust relationship happens in one place instead of a dozen. Federation is what keeps identity from fragmenting into a hundred disconnected credential stores, each of which is a place a former employee's access quietly survives.

Here is what this topic actually buys you. Once you hold identity as credential plus scope plus lifetime, and once you keep human and machine actors in separate mental buckets, you can walk into any cloud provider's IAM console — whatever they've named their primitives — and immediately locate where the real decisions are being made. You stop asking "how do I make this permission error go away" and start asking "what is the smallest scope that makes this work, how short can this credential's life be, and what breaks if it leaks anyway." That reframe is the difference between an IAM setup that merely works and one that's still defensible the morning after something goes wrong.

## Level 2 candidates

**Machine identity** — Covers how code acting as an identity differs from a human doing so, and why the same authentication mechanisms and assumptions can't safely serve both. Worth deeper exploration because the machine case is where over-provisioning, key exfiltration, and replay attacks concentrate, and it's the identity type most teams model laziest.

**Least-privilege policy design** — Covers the practical craft of scoping permissions to the minimum a workload actually needs, and the operational-friction-versus-blast-radius tradeoff that governs every such decision. Worth going deeper because the gap between the principle and its real implementation — role explosion, permission debugging, drift over time — is where most organizations actually fail.

**Secrets and credential rotation** — Covers why a credential's lifetime is the dimension that turns a leak from a catastrophe into an inconvenience, and the mechanics of issuing, expiring, and rotating credentials. Worth a deep dive because rotation is operationally hard in ways the concept doesn't reveal, and the failure modes (rotation that breaks live systems, secrets that can't be rotated without downtime) are their own discipline.

**Federation and delegated trust** — Covers how an organization vouches for identities across providers and systems instead of re-creating identity everywhere, and how trust relationships are established and revoked. Worth exploring because the trust-chain mechanics and their failure modes — stale trust, over-broad delegation, orphaned access surviving offboarding — are subtle and high-consequence.
