# Data & Integration

The moment you split one system into two, you inherit a problem you didn't have before — and it's not the problem most people expect. It isn't that the code gets harder to write; each service, in isolation, is usually simpler than the monolith it came from. The problem is that two things which no longer share memory, a database transaction, or a deploy cycle now have to stay coordinated across a network that can drop, delay, or reorder anything you send. A monolith gets coordination for free: a function call either happens or the whole process crashes together. The instant you draw a boundary between services, that free guarantee evaporates, and everything in this domain exists to buy some fraction of it back.

So the first mental model to hold is that integration is not a feature you add to a distributed system — it's the tax you pay for having one. Every messaging layer, every queue, every API contract is there because you chose independence, and independence has a cost denominated in coordination. Once you see it that way, you stop asking "which messaging technology should I use" and start asking the question underneath it: what am I willing to give up — latency, simplicity, certainty about ordering — in exchange for the two systems not needing to know about each other's internals?

The central fork in the whole domain is synchronous versus asynchronous communication, and it's worth being precise about what actually differs, because the difference is not speed. When a caller waits for a response — synchronous — it couples its own reliability to the reliability of everything downstream. If the service it calls is slow, the caller is slow. If that service calls a third one that's down, the failure propagates back up the chain, and one struggling component at the bottom can take down five above it. Synchronous communication is easy to reason about precisely because it behaves like the function call it's imitating: you ask, you wait, you get an answer or an error. That legibility is exactly what makes it dangerous at scale, because it quietly stitches independent services back into a single fragile unit that fails together.

Asynchronous communication breaks that coupling by letting the caller hand off a message and move on without waiting. This is strictly better for resilience — a slow or dead consumer no longer stalls the producer — but it doesn't make the coordination problem disappear. It relocates it, and usually to a harder place. The moment you stop waiting for a response, you've committed to answering a question the synchronous model never forced on you: what happens to a message that fails when the code that sent it is long gone and has already moved on to other work? That message is now orphaned work, floating two removes from anyone who knows what it was for. The entire discipline of failure handling in asynchronous systems — retries, dead-letter queues, idempotency — exists to answer that one question, and teams that adopt async messaging for its resilience benefits routinely discover they've traded a visible failure mode for an invisible one.

This is why event-driven and pub-sub designs are more consequential than they first appear. When you decouple a producer from its consumers — the producer emits an event and doesn't know or care who's listening — you change what "the system is down" even means. There's no longer a single call chain you can trace from request to failure; there's a fan-out of independent consumers, any of which might be behind, stuck, or silently dropping work while everything else looks healthy. The system can be "up" by every dashboard you have and still be failing to do something a user asked for an hour ago. That's the price of decoupling: you gain the ability to add and remove consumers without touching the producer, and you lose the simple mental model of a request that either succeeds or fails in front of you.

There's a second axis to this domain that has almost nothing to do with networks and almost everything to do with people. API contracts and schemas exist so that two teams can change their systems independently without a phone call. That sounds like a technical concern, but it's really an organizational one: the contract is the agreement that lets one team ship on Tuesday and the other ship on Thursday without either breaking the other. When teams don't version their contracts deliberately — when a producer changes a field's meaning and assumes everyone downstream will just cope — you get the class of outage where nothing crashed, no error was thrown, and yet the data quietly went wrong across a boundary nobody was watching. Contract discipline is what turns "we need to coordinate every release" into "we can each move at our own pace," and the absence of it is why some organizations find their independent services are, in practice, welded together and must be deployed as a set.

Two more boundaries are worth naming because they protect the system from pressures the individual services can't see. An API gateway with rate limiting is the boundary that protects a system from its own popularity — it's the thing that decides a sudden flood of traffic gets throttled at the edge rather than allowed to overwhelm every service behind it. And dead-letter handling is the boundary that decides what happens to work that genuinely cannot be completed. The tempting default — silently drop it — is almost always worse than the alternatives, because dropped work is work you can't audit, retry, or even prove was lost. A message in a dead-letter queue is a problem you can see; a message that vanished is a problem you'll discover from an angry customer.

The skill this domain builds is the ability to look at a distributed system and predict where it will break under change — not under load, under change. Load failures are often obvious and get engineered for. The subtle failures live at the seams: the synchronous call that coupled two services nobody meant to couple, the schema change that broke a consumer three teams away, the async message that failed and went nowhere because no one designed for its failure. Once you see integration as the question of how independently-deployed things stay coordinated, you stop being surprised by these. You start reading a system's architecture as a map of its coupling, and coupling is where the next incident is already waiting.

## Level 2 candidates

**Synchronous vs. asynchronous communication** — Covers the coupling tradeoff at the heart of the domain: whether a caller waits for downstream systems and thereby inherits their reliability, or hands off and inherits a harder failure-handling problem instead. Worth going deeper because the choice determines whether one slow service can cascade into five others, and the second-order consequences (idempotency, ordering, backpressure) are where most real architectures get it wrong.

**Pub-sub and event-driven design** — Covers how decoupling producers from consumers changes the shape of the system and, with it, the definition of "down." Worth a deep dive because the fan-out model breaks the intuitive request-and-response mental model entirely, and reasoning about partial failure across many independent consumers is a genuinely different skill.

**Dead-letter and failure handling** — Covers what happens to work that can't be completed, and why silent dropping is worse than nearly every alternative. Worth exploring because the design of retries, backoff, poison-message handling, and dead-letter queues is where asynchronous systems either become auditable or become black boxes that lose work invisibly.

**API gateways and rate limiting** — Covers the boundary that protects a system from its own traffic, deciding what gets throttled at the edge before it can overwhelm the services behind it. Worth going deeper because rate limiting, quotas, and gateway policy sit at the intersection of reliability, cost, and abuse prevention, and the mechanisms have real tradeoffs worth understanding.

**Schema and contract versioning** — Covers how two teams change their services independently without breaking each other, and what happens when they skip the discipline. Worth a deep dive because contract evolution (backward and forward compatibility, versioning strategies, breaking-change detection) is as much an organizational practice as a technical one, and the failure mode — quiet data corruption across a boundary — is among the hardest to detect after the fact.
