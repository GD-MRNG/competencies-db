# L1-09 · Cost & Resource Management

Your cloud bill is not a finance document. It is an architecture diagram written in dollars — a second, unflattering description of every decision you and your team have made about how work gets done. Every line item traces back to a resource someone provisioned, a service someone left running, a data path someone chose. The engineer who picked an instance type made a financial decision. The one who wrote a query that scans a whole table made one too. Most of them didn't know it at the time, and that unawareness is the actual problem this domain exists to solve. Cost surprises are almost never a pricing mystery; they're an architecture you can't see clearly, showing up somewhere you're finally forced to look.

The single most useful distinction to hold in your head is the split between provisioned cost and consumption cost. Provisioned means you pay for capacity whether or not you use it — a reserved instance, a database sitting at ten percent utilization, a fixed pool of nodes humming along at 3am with nothing to do. Consumption means you pay only for what you actually use — a function invoked ten thousand times, a gigabyte transferred, a query executed. Neither is cheaper in the abstract. They're bets on different usage shapes. Provisioned wins when load is steady and predictable, because you can commit ahead and buy the discount. Consumption wins when load is spiky or uncertain, because you're not paying for the valleys. The trap is that these are not permanent properties of a workload — a service that fit consumption pricing beautifully at launch can grow into a steady, high-volume workhorse that would be far cheaper provisioned, and nobody notices because the bill went up gradually and the architecture never changed.

That mismatch — a workload running on the wrong pricing model for the shape it actually has — is where most "why is this so expensive" conversations end up, once you dig past the sticker shock. The fix is rarely a discount negotiation. It's recognizing that the shape of your usage and the shape of your pricing have drifted apart, and realigning them. This is why cost work is engineering work, not procurement work. You can't fix a model mismatch with a spreadsheet; you fix it by changing what the system does or how it's provisioned. The invoice is telling you something structural, and the skill is learning to read it as a signal about design rather than a number to be haggled down.

Then there's the quieter problem, the one that doesn't announce itself: attribution. Knowing what a cost is, is easy — the provider itemizes it. Knowing whose it is, which team, which feature, which product decision, is the hard part, and it sounds like bureaucratic overhead right up until you try to answer the question "is this worth it?" without it. That question is unanswerable in aggregate. A single monthly total tells you nothing about whether the analytics pipeline is earning its keep or the staging environment nobody's used in three months is quietly costing more than a headcount. Attribution is what turns a lump sum into a set of decisions you can actually evaluate. Without it, cost has no owner, and cost without an owner is cost that never gets fixed — because "someone should look at that" is not a person, and the bill belongs to everyone and therefore no one.

The failure mode this all produces is a slow one, which is what makes it dangerous. Nobody provisions a wildly oversized fleet on purpose. What happens instead is accumulation: an instance sized generously "to be safe" during a launch and never revisited, a snapshot retention policy that quietly hoards storage for years, an orphaned load balancer pointing at a service that was decommissioned two quarters ago. Each individual decision was defensible in the moment. Together they form a layer of spend that no single person authorized and no single person is watching. The instinct to over-provision as a safety margin is itself a recurring cost, not a one-time buffer — you pay for that headroom every hour, forever, and "forever" is a long time to pay for capacity you guessed you might need.

The reframe that makes all of this tractable is to stop treating cost as something the finance team owns after the fact and start treating it as an architectural signal available in real time. A cost line growing faster than the traffic that justifies it is a design smell, the same way a function that's hard to test or a class with too many responsibilities is a design smell. It's the system telling you that something is misaligned — a wrong pricing model, an untagged resource, a data path more expensive than anyone intended. Once you can read the bill this way, waste stops being an abstract efficiency mandate handed down from above and becomes something you can see and fix from inside the architecture, where the decision was made in the first place.

That's the whole skill, and it's worth stating plainly: the practitioner who understands cost isn't the one who knows the cheapest instance type. It's the one who can look at a bill and reverse-engineer the architecture that produced it, spot where the shape of the spend has drifted from the shape of the value, and name the specific decision that needs to change. Everything else in this domain — the pricing models, the tagging schemes, the rightsizing exercises — is machinery in service of that one move: making an invisible design decision visible before it becomes an invoice you have to explain.

## Level 2 candidates

**Provisioned vs. consumption pricing** — Covers the core tension between paying for reserved capacity regardless of use and paying only for what you consume, and how to match each to the workload shape it actually fits. Worth deeper exploration because the mismatch between model and workload is the root of most cost surprises, and diagnosing it requires understanding usage curves, commitment discounts, and the break-even math where one model overtakes the other.

**Egress and data-transfer pricing** — Covers why moving data out of a provider is priced differently from moving it in, and how that asymmetry works. Worth going deeper because this pricing structure is the real mechanism behind vendor lock-in — a structural constraint on architecture, not a technical limitation — and it catches teams off guard in ways compute and storage pricing rarely do.

**Rightsizing** — Covers the practice of matching provisioned capacity to actual demand rather than to a worst-case guess. Worth exploring because the "provision generously to be safe" instinct is so common and so intuitively reasonable that recognizing it as a recurring cost rather than a prudent buffer requires unlearning a default, and doing it well means understanding utilization data and headroom tradeoffs.

**Idle and orphaned resources** — Covers how spend accumulates from resources nobody is actively using or even aware still exist. Worth deeper treatment because the detection and prevention of silent accumulation is a genuine operational discipline — it involves lifecycle tracking, ownership, and the organizational question of how resources get created faster than they get cleaned up.

**Tagging and attribution** — Covers the mechanics of assigning costs to teams, features, and products so that spend has an owner. Worth going deeper because attribution is the precondition for every "is this worth it" decision, and building a tagging scheme that survives contact with a real organization is a harder design problem than it first appears.

**Quotas and budget guardrails** — Covers the difference between controls that prevent a costly mistake before it happens and ones that only report it after the money is spent. Worth exploring because the preventive-versus-detective distinction determines whether a cost control actually protects you or merely documents the damage, and designing guardrails that block without paralyzing legitimate work is a real tradeoff.
