# Observability

You already have logs, metrics, and traces. Every provider hands them to you by default now — the dashboards populate themselves, the log streams fill up, the trace waterfalls render whether you asked for them or not. So if collecting this data were the skill, observability would be a solved problem, and you would not be reading this while something is on fire and you cannot tell why. The skill was never collection. The skill is knowing which of the three to look at first, and that skill only comes from understanding that these are not three tools that happen to ship together — they are three answers to three different questions, and reaching for the wrong one during an incident costs you the minutes that matter most.

Start with the questions, because the tools make no sense until you do. When something goes wrong, you are really asking three things in sequence, and each has a dedicated instrument. Is it broken, and how broken — that is metrics, the numbers that describe the system over time: request rates, error counts, latency percentiles, queue depths. What actually happened, in specific detail, at a specific moment — that is logs, the discrete record of events as the system emitted them. And where, in a chain of services that all had a hand in a single request, did the thing that broke actually break — that is tracing, which stitches one request's journey across every service it touched into a single view. Metrics tell you the temperature. Logs tell you what was said. Traces tell you which room the fire started in.

The reason tracing exists as its own category — rather than being something you could reconstruct from logs — is the single most important thing to internalize here, because it explains why the old way of debugging quietly stopped working. For a long time, debugging in production meant something concrete: a server is misbehaving, so you log into that server, read its logs, and find the problem where it lives. That model rests on an assumption you probably never noticed you were making — that a request is handled in one place. The moment a single incoming request started fanning out across a dozen independent services, each with its own logs on its own host, that assumption collapsed. Now the request that failed for your user touched a gateway, an auth service, three internal APIs, a database, and a cache, and the log line that explains the failure is sitting on one of those hosts with nothing to tie it back to the others. Tracing exists to reattach those pieces — to give every service that touched a request a shared identifier, so that "this one request was slow" resolves into "this one request was slow because the fourth service in the chain waited two seconds on a database call." Without that, you are reading a dozen unrelated log streams and guessing.

Metrics and logs pull in opposite directions, and knowing which way each leans tells you when to reach for it. Metrics are cheap to store and fast to query because they throw away detail on purpose — a metric does not remember which request was slow, only that the ninety-fifth percentile latency rose at 3:14. That aggregation is exactly what makes metrics the right first instrument: they tell you something is wrong and roughly how wrong, across the whole system, at a glance, which is what an alert fires on. Logs are the opposite bargain. They keep the detail — the specific error, the specific input, the specific stack trace — which is what you need once you already know where to look, but that detail is expensive to store and slow to search across a fleet. The failure mode of reaching for them in the wrong order is familiar: you start grepping logs during an incident before you have any idea which service or which time window to grep, and you drown in volume while the clock runs.

That ordering — metrics to detect, traces to localize, logs to diagnose — is the working loop, and it is worth holding onto because incidents are precisely when clear thinking is hardest. Metrics page you and tell you the system is degraded. Traces narrow a degraded system down to the one service or the one dependency actually responsible, so you are no longer suspecting all twelve. Logs, once you know where to stand, tell you the specific thing that went wrong there. Skip a step and you pay for it: jump straight to logs and you are searching everywhere; jump straight to traces without metrics and you may not even know there is anything to trace yet.

Two cautions keep this from being tidier than it is in practice. The first is that observability data is only as good as the discipline behind it — an unstructured log line that a human can read but a machine cannot query becomes useless the instant you have more than one instance producing it, and a metric that describes the system tells you nothing about when a human should actually be woken up. The second is the counterintuitive truth about alerting: more alerts produce worse incident response, not better. Past a certain volume, every additional alert is noise that trains people to ignore the channel, so the alert that matters arrives and dies unread. Detection is not about instrumenting more; it is about instrumenting the few things whose failure genuinely warrants a human, and staying quiet about the rest.

So when you evaluate any provider's observability offering — and the product names will differ everywhere you go — do not ask how much it collects, because they all collect plenty. Ask which of the three questions it actually answers well. Some tools give you gorgeous metric dashboards and a tracing story that falls apart the moment your architecture gets interesting. Some log everything and leave you to reconstruct causality by hand. The provider's marketing will not tell you which question it is weak on; your next incident will. Learn to see the three questions underneath the products, and every observability tool you ever touch sorts itself instantly into what it can tell you and what it cannot.

## Level 2 candidates

**Structured logging** — Covers why logs need to be machine-parseable records with consistent fields rather than free-form text lines. Worth going deeper because the transition from single-instance to multi-instance systems is exactly where unstructured logs silently become unqueryable, and most teams don't feel the pain until they're already drowning in it.

**Metrics and SLIs/SLOs** — Covers the difference between a number that merely describes the system and a number that defines when it is unacceptably broken. Worth going deeper because the leap from "we have metrics" to "we have thresholds that mean something and page someone" is where observability stops being decorative and starts driving decisions.

**Distributed tracing** — Covers how a shared request identifier lets you attribute a single slow request to the one service actually responsible across a long call chain. Worth going deeper because the mechanics of propagating trace context and sampling intelligently are non-obvious, and tracing is the instrument most often collected but least often used well.

**Alert design** — Covers why alert volume and incident-response quality move in opposite directions past a threshold, and how to decide what actually warrants a human. Worth going deeper because this is a discipline problem more than a tooling one, and it's where good observability data most commonly gets wasted.

**Push vs. pull telemetry** — Covers the architectural choice between systems that must be scraped for their data and systems that ship it outward. Worth going deeper because the tradeoff shapes how your telemetry behaves under network partitions and scale, and it's a decision baked into tooling that's hard to reverse later.
