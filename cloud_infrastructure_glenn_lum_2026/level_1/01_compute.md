# L1-01 · Compute

You already know how to run code on a machine. What you may not have internalized is that every compute product you've ever used — every VM, container platform, and serverless function — is the same machine with a different amount of it hidden from you. The industry doesn't sell you fundamentally different things when it sells you EC2, Fargate, and Lambda. It sells you the same underlying problem — code needs a place to run — packaged with progressively more of the operational burden absorbed, and progressively less of the control handed back. Once you see compute this way, the bewildering catalog of products across every provider collapses into a single axis, and your job becomes locating any given product on it.

That axis is a trade of control for velocity, and it moves in one direction. At one end sits the virtual machine: you get an operating system, and with it the freedom to install anything, tune anything, and run anything — but you also own patching, hardening, and the uncomfortable knowledge that a machine left running is a machine you're paying for whether it's doing work or not. Move up a step to containers and you trade away the machine's specificity for portability: the same image runs anywhere, but now you've inherited a new job the VM never handed you — orchestration, the problem of deciding which container runs where, how many, and what happens when one dies. Move up again to functions, and you give up both the OS and the orchestration entirely. You write a unit of logic, the provider runs it on demand, and you pay per invocation. What you get is the least operational ownership available. What you pay for it is cold starts — the latency penalty of a function that wasn't already running when the request arrived — and a near-total loss of visibility into where and how your code executes.

The single most useful move you can make with any compute product is to find the line it draws between what the provider manages and what you still own. This line is not a detail. It is where outages come from, where cost surprises come from, and where security gaps come from — because everything on your side of the line is your responsibility whether you realized you'd accepted it or not, and everything on their side is a black box you cannot fix when it breaks. "Serverless" is the cleanest illustration of how this line deceives people. There are still servers. There are always servers. What's gone is your visibility into them — and visibility is exactly what you want back at 3am when something is wrong and the layer that failed is one you're contractually forbidden from inspecting. The abstraction didn't remove the server; it removed your ability to look at it.

This is why the shared responsibility boundary is worth naming explicitly: it redraws itself every time you move along the axis. With a VM, "the provider handles it" covers the hardware and the hypervisor, and roughly nothing above that. With a managed container service, the boundary climbs to include the orchestration layer. With a function, it climbs again to include nearly everything except your code and its configuration. The trap is assuming the boundary is fixed when it moves with every product decision. A team that hardens its VMs diligently and then adopts a function platform without re-asking "what's ours now?" hasn't eliminated its security work — it's just stopped doing the part it forgot was still there, or is now paying attention to a layer the provider actually owns.

Two properties of any compute product become predictable the moment you've placed it on the axis. The first is its failure mode around state. When an instance crashes, does it come back as the same instance with its data intact, or as a fresh one with everything since the last save gone? This is not an accident of the platform — it's a design decision baked into where the product sits. Ephemeral compute (most containers, all functions) assumes state lives elsewhere, and punishes you brutally if you assumed otherwise. The difference between a crash you recover from and a crash that loses data is a choice someone made, usually before you arrived. The second predictable property is scaling behavior. Autoscaling that triggers on CPU works beautifully for CPU-bound work and fails silently for I/O-bound work — a service waiting on a slow database shows low CPU while drowning, so the platform sees no reason to add capacity and the queue backs up invisibly. Knowing what a product measures to decide when to scale tells you which workloads it will quietly mishandle.

The last thing the axis explains is price, which is really a price for control surrendered. Providers sell you the same physical hardware at wildly different rates depending on how much say you're willing to give up over when you get to use it. Reserved capacity — you commit, you pay a fixed rate, the machine is yours — costs more per hour than interruptible capacity, where the provider can reclaim the machine with little warning because you agreed it could. That price gap isn't a discount for loyalty; it's the market price of control. And underneath even that sits tenant isolation: the question of what physically stops another customer's workload from touching yours. The uncomfortable truth is that this guarantee gets weaker as density and cost efficiency go up. The cheaper and more packed the compute, the more you're sharing, and the more "noisy neighbor" effects — someone else's workload stealing the resources you thought were guaranteed — become your problem despite capacity you were promised.

So the skill this topic builds is not operating any particular compute product. You already do that. The skill is prediction: given a compute product you've never touched, placing it on the control-for-velocity axis lets you forecast its failure modes, its state behavior, its scaling blind spots, its security surface, and its pricing logic before you've run a single workload on it. Products are vendor-specific and endless. The axis is universal and short. Learn the axis, and the products stop being things to memorize and become things to locate.

## Level 2 candidates

**Provisioning models** — Covers the spectrum from self-managed VMs to fully serverless functions and what visibility each surrenders. Worth going deeper because "serverless isn't serverless" is a mental model with real operational consequences, and the exact mechanics of cold starts and hidden servers reward a closer look than a Level 1 framing allows.

**Scaling triggers** — Covers what a platform measures to decide when to add or remove capacity, and why CPU-based triggers fail for I/O-bound workloads. Worth a deep dive because the failure is silent — the system looks healthy by the metric it's watching while degrading by the metric that matters — and diagnosing it requires understanding the mechanism.

**Instance lifecycle** — Covers what survives a crash and what doesn't, and why ephemeral versus durable state is a design decision rather than a platform quirk. Worth exploring because the difference between recoverable and unrecoverable failure is invisible until it isn't, and the assumptions are usually inherited rather than chosen.

**Interruptible vs. reserved capacity** — Covers the economic logic behind selling identical hardware at different prices based on how much control you cede. Worth going deeper because the pricing structure is a direct readout of the provider's incentives, and understanding it turns capacity purchasing from guesswork into a deliberate trade.

**Shared responsibility boundary** — Covers how the line between provider-managed and self-managed shifts with every step up the abstraction stack. Worth a deep dive because the boundary's movement is precisely where security and operational gaps hide, and re-asking "what's ours now?" at each layer is a discipline that prevents whole classes of incidents.

**Tenant isolation guarantees** — Covers what physically separates your workload from another customer's, and why that separation weakens as density rises. Worth exploring because noisy-neighbor effects and isolation boundaries are the part of "guaranteed capacity" that the guarantee quietly doesn't cover.
