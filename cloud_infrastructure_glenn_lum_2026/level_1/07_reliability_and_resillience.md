# L1-07 · Reliability & Resilience

Most teams describe their reliability posture in the language of uptime — three nines, four nines, a percentage on a status page. It sounds precise, and it is almost entirely useless. Uptime tells you how often the system was working in the past; it tells you nothing about what happens the next time something breaks, or how far the damage spreads when it does. The reframe that separates people who operate resilient systems from people who merely hope theirs stay up is this: failure is not a probability you drive toward zero, it's an event you plan the containment of in advance. You will not prevent the disk from filling, the zone from going dark, or the downstream dependency from timing out. What you get to decide — today, while nothing is on fire — is what else goes down when it does.

That reframe changes the question you ask of every reliability feature. Instead of "does this make us more reliable," which is unanswerable, you ask "what does this actually contain?" Every reliability pattern is a pre-negotiated answer to a failure that hasn't happened yet, and every one of them costs you something now — money, latency, complexity, operational surface — in exchange for a smaller blast radius later. If you can't name the failure a pattern is negotiating against, you haven't bought resilience; you've bought overhead. The discipline is not accumulating patterns, it's matching each one to the specific failure it contains and being honest about the ones you've chosen to leave uncontained.

Blast radius is the concept that makes all of this concrete. When something fails, blast radius is the set of everything that fails with it — the users affected, the services that depend on it, the data that becomes unreachable. "Highly available" without a defined blast radius is a marketing claim, because availability that isn't bounded is just an assertion that failure won't happen, which is the one thing you already know is false. A system with a small, well-understood blast radius can fail constantly and still feel reliable to its users, because each failure is contained to a corner nobody notices. A system with a large or unknown blast radius can run flawlessly for a year and then take everything down at once. The percentage on the status page can't tell these two systems apart. Blast radius can.

The tactical patterns you'll encounter are all blast-radius tools, and they cut in more than one direction — which is exactly why you have to understand what each contains rather than sprinkling them everywhere. Retries are the clearest example. A retry with sensible backoff turns a transient blip into a non-event: the request that failed once succeeds on the second attempt and no human ever knows. But the same mechanism, applied naively, becomes a retry storm — every client hammering a struggling service in unison, turning a brief degradation into a full outage by burying the thing you were trying to protect under the weight of your own optimism. Circuit breakers exist precisely because retries have this failure mode: a breaker stops sending requests to a dependency that's clearly unhealthy, containing the damage to the callers instead of amplifying it downstream. Retries and circuit breakers are two halves of the same decision about what happens at the boundary between a caller and a failing dependency.

Redundancy is the same story at the infrastructure layer, and it's where the most expensive mistakes hide. The instinct is right — run more than one of everything so a single failure doesn't take you down — but redundancy only works if the copies don't share a failure domain. Two instances in the same availability zone, drawing the same power and sitting behind the same network path, are not redundant; they're one failure wearing two names, and they will go down together the moment their shared dependency does. This is why "multi-zone" and "multi-region" are meaningful distinctions rather than marketing tiers: they describe how much of the physical world your copies actually have in common. Redundancy that shares a failure domain isn't redundancy at all — it's the appearance of it, which is worse than none, because it lets you believe you're protected against exactly the failure that will get you.

Then there are the two numbers most teams don't know until an incident forces them to find out: RPO and RTO. Recovery Point Objective is how much data you're willing to lose — the gap between your last good backup and the moment of failure. Recovery Time Objective is how long you're willing to be down while you recover. These matter because "we have backups" is a statement with no operational content until you attach numbers to it. A backup that runs nightly gives you an RPO of up to twenty-four hours — a full day of data gone — which is either completely fine or completely catastrophic depending entirely on what the data is, and that's a business decision, not a technical one. The value of RPO and RTO isn't the recovery; it's that stating them out loud turns a vague claim ("we have a disaster recovery plan") into a testable commitment someone has actually agreed to. Most teams discover the gap between their assumed RPO and their real one during the incident, which is the worst possible time to learn it.

Which points at the quiet through-line of this entire domain: a resilience assumption that has never been tested under failure is indistinguishable from hope. You believe the failover works. You believe the backup restores. You believe the circuit breaker trips before the retry storm forms. Until you've forced each of those under real failure conditions — deliberately, on your schedule, with people watching — they are beliefs, not properties of the system. This is what chaos testing and load testing are for: not to prove the system is reliable, but to convert your assumptions into either confirmed guarantees or known gaps before an incident does the converting for you, at 3am, in front of your users.

So the skill this domain builds isn't memorizing patterns — every provider ships retries, health checks, and multi-zone options, and you can look up how to configure them. The skill is thinking in blast radius instead of uptime. Once you do, you can walk up to any resilience feature, in any provider, under any product name, and ask the only question that matters: what failure does this contain, what does it cost me to run, and have I actually tested that it does what I think? Reliability isn't the absence of failure. It's knowing, before it happens, exactly how much of your system a given failure is allowed to take with it.

## Level 2 candidates

**Blast radius** — Covers how to define and shrink the set of things that fail together when any single component goes down. Worth going deeper because it's the concept every other reliability pattern is ultimately serving, and reasoning about it explicitly is what separates a bounded failure from a cascading one.

**Retries, backoff, and circuit breakers** — Covers the mechanics of caller-side failure handling, including the exponential backoff and jitter that keep retries from synchronizing into a storm. Worth a deep dive because the line between a retry that saves you and a retry that takes down the thing you were protecting is a matter of specific configuration, not intent.

**Redundancy across zones and regions** — Covers what it means for two copies to truly not share a failure domain, and the latency and cost tradeoffs of pushing redundancy from zone to region. Worth exploring because the most common redundancy failure is redundancy that only looks redundant, and spotting shared failure domains is a non-obvious skill.

**RPO and RTO** — Covers how to derive, state, and validate the two recovery numbers, and how backup and replication strategies map onto them. Worth going deeper because turning these from assumed values into tested commitments involves concrete architectural choices most teams never make until an incident forces the question.

**Chaos and load testing** — Covers the practice of deliberately inducing failure and load to convert resilience assumptions into confirmed guarantees or known gaps. Worth a dive because doing it safely and usefully — blast-radius-limited, observable, and repeatable — is its own discipline, and it's the only thing that distinguishes tested resilience from hope.
