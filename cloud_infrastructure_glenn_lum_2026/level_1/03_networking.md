# L1-03 · Networking

When a request fails in production, your instinct is to open the code. But the failure is almost never inside the code itself — it's in the path the request took to reach the code, or the path the response took to return. This is why networking is the most common blind spot for engineers moving deeper into operational work, and the most consequential one: you can read every line of a service and still have no idea why it's slow, or why it's suddenly reachable from the open internet. The network is the part of the system you didn't write, can't see in your editor, and are nonetheless fully responsible for.

Strip away the product names — VPCs, security groups, subnets, peering connections, transit gateways, whatever a given provider calls them — and every cloud network is answering one question, repeated at every layer: what is the default posture? Open until closed, or closed until opened? That's it. That single choice, made over and over at the instance level, the subnet level, the account level, and the organization level, determines whether your system is private by default or exposed by default. Providers didn't converge on private-by-default virtual networks because it was elegant. They converged on it because the alternative produced a decade of breaches that were architecture failures, not code failures — databases reachable from the internet, storage buckets open to anyone who guessed the name, internal services that assumed the network around them was trusted and were wrong. Once you see the network as a stack of trust boundaries, every "why is this exposed" question becomes a search for the one boundary that was left open when it should have been closed.

The second thing the network is made of is distance, and distance is physics, not configuration. Latency is not a performance detail you tune in code — it's a floor set by how far your data center sits from your users, and no amount of optimization moves the speed of light. This is why the location of your infrastructure is an architectural decision, not a deployment afterthought. When you understand networking as trust boundaries plus physical distance, nearly every operational question about the network resolves into one of those two things: it's either a trust boundary that's wrong (something is exposed, or something can't reach something it needs to) or it's distance (something is slow because bytes are traveling farther than you assumed).

Segmentation is where the trust-boundary idea becomes concrete. Early cloud adoption defaulted to a single flat network — everything could talk to everything, and "inside the network" was treated as "safe." That was the default failure mode, because it means one compromised component has a clear path to every other component. Segmentation replaced it: you carve the network into zones so that being inside one zone grants you nothing about the next. The discipline isn't about drawing lines for their own sake; it's about ensuring that when something is breached, the breach is contained to a segment rather than free to move laterally across your entire footprint. A flat network turns a small compromise into a total one.

Routing and load balancing are where the network decides not just whether traffic arrives but how it's distributed when it does. The distinction worth holding onto here is between routing failure and routing degradation. A routing failure is clean and loud — nothing answers, and you know immediately. Routing degradation is the dangerous one: something answers, but slowly, or intermittently, or only for some fraction of requests, because a load balancer is still sending traffic to an instance that's technically alive but effectively broken. The second kind is harder to detect and harder to reason about, because the system looks up while behaving as though it's down. Understanding what your load balancer considers "healthy" is often the difference between an incident you catch and one that quietly bleeds.

Access control at the network layer is the blunt-instrument version of the trust-boundary question. Firewall rules — allow this port, from this range, to this destination — operate on addresses and ports, not on identity. They can't tell you *who* is connecting, only *where from*. That's why identity-based access is the sharper tool: it answers "is this caller allowed" rather than "is this caller coming from an address I've decided to trust." But network-layer controls don't go away just because identity-based access exists. They're the outer wall, the coarse filter that keeps the obvious garbage out before anything more sophisticated has to make a decision. You need both, and knowing which one a given rule belongs to keeps you from mistaking a network allow-list for an actual authorization model.

DNS is the layer nobody thinks about until it takes everything down at once. It's the translation between names and addresses, and service discovery is the same idea inside your own system — how one service finds the current address of another when instances are constantly being created and destroyed. What makes DNS uniquely dangerous is that when it fails, it doesn't fail in one place. Every system that depends on resolving a name fails simultaneously, which means a DNS problem presents as *everything is broken at once* — the single most misleading signal in an incident, because it points you everywhere and nowhere. Learning to check name resolution early is one of those habits that separates a fast diagnosis from an hour of chasing ghosts.

Finally, regions and zones are where distance becomes a design decision with a built-in conflict. Spreading across zones or regions buys you resilience — if one physical location fails, another survives. But it also introduces distance, and distance is latency, and the two pull in opposite directions. The more you spread out for resilience, the farther your components are from each other, and the more every cross-location call costs you in time. There is no setting that gives you both maximum resilience and minimum latency; there is only the tradeoff, made deliberately or made by accident. The engineers who make it deliberately are the ones who understood that "multi-region" was never just a reliability checkbox — it was a latency decision wearing a reliability costume.

The skill this builds is a kind of triage reflex. When something in production is slow, or exposed, or unreachable, you stop reaching for the code first. You ask which of two things it is: a trust boundary set wrong, or bytes traveling too far. Almost everything the network throws at you is one of those two, and once you can sort a symptom into the right bucket in the first thirty seconds, the network stops being the part of the system you're afraid of.

## Level 2 candidates

**Isolation and segmentation** — Covers how a network is carved into zones so that compromising one part grants no automatic access to the rest. Worth going deeper because the shift from flat networks to segmented ones is the clearest example of blast-radius thinking applied to network design, and the details of how segmentation is enforced vary enough between providers to be genuinely confusing.

**Traffic routing and load balancing** — Covers how traffic is distributed across instances and, critically, how "healthy" is defined and detected. Worth a deep dive because routing degradation — something answering slowly rather than not at all — is one of the hardest failure modes to detect, and health-check design is where that battle is won or lost.

**Access control at the network layer** — Covers the difference between address-and-port-based firewall rules and identity-based access, and when each is the right tool. Worth exploring because the interaction between coarse network controls and fine-grained identity controls is where a lot of "we thought this was locked down" incidents originate.

**DNS and service discovery** — Covers name-to-address translation and how services find each other in an environment where instances constantly change. Worth going deeper because DNS failures present as total, simultaneous outages that mislead every diagnosis, and understanding the resolution path is a disproportionately high-leverage debugging skill.

**Regions, zones, and physical locality** — Covers how physical placement of infrastructure trades resilience against latency. Worth a deep dive because "multi-region" is routinely adopted as a reliability feature without accounting for the latency cost it imposes, and the tradeoff is only manageable once you can see both sides of it explicitly.
