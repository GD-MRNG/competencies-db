# L1-10 · Terminal Multiplexing, Remote Sessions, and SSH

The moment your work involves a machine that isn't physically in front of you, the layered model you've been building stops being a tidy abstraction and starts paying actual rent. Remote work is the scenario that most aggressively exposes whether you understand which of the three layers — emulator, shell, kernel — is responsible for what, because remote work physically separates them across two machines and a network. If you've been treating "the terminal" as a single thing, SSH will feel like magic that sometimes betrays you: a long-running job dies when you close your laptop, a session hangs when your WiFi blinks, a window full of output you wanted to read disappears the instant the connection drops. None of that is mysterious. It's the predictable consequence of which layer lives where.

Start with what SSH actually is. When you SSH into a server, your local terminal emulator does not change roles — it is still doing exactly what it always does, drawing text and forwarding keystrokes. What changes is where the shell is. Instead of your emulator talking to a local shell process on your laptop, it's talking, through an encrypted network connection, to a shell process running on the remote machine, against the remote machine's kernel, seeing the remote machine's filesystem and processes. Every keystroke you type travels across the network to that remote shell; every byte of output travels back to be drawn by your local emulator. SSH is not a remote desktop, not a file sync, not a "session" in any rich sense — it is a pipe between a local emulator and a remote shell, and that's the entire trick.

This reframing immediately explains the most common piece of remote-session pain: closing your laptop kills your long-running job on the server. The shell on the remote machine is a process, and like every process it has a notion of a controlling terminal — the thing it's reading input from and writing output to. When your SSH connection dies, the remote shell loses its controlling terminal, the kernel notices, and it sends signals (typically SIGHUP, the "hangup" signal, a fossil from the days when terminals were physical devices on phone lines that could literally hang up) down the process tree. By default, processes that receive SIGHUP terminate. Your eight-hour training run, your slow database migration, your `apt upgrade` — gone, not because the remote machine failed, but because the chain of "I am attached to a terminal" snapped, and the kernel cleaned up everything that was depending on that terminal.

Once you see the failure mode clearly, the tools that exist to fix it stop looking like a grab bag and start looking like two distinct strategies. The lightweight strategy is to detach individual processes from the controlling terminal so the SIGHUP never reaches them. That's what `nohup` does when you launch a command — it arranges for the process to ignore SIGHUP — and what `disown` does after the fact, removing a job from the shell's list of things it'll send SIGHUP to on exit. These are surgical: they protect one process, and you lose the ability to interact with it after detaching. The heavyweight strategy is a terminal multiplexer — tmux or screen — which inserts an entirely new layer between your SSH session and the shells you actually work in.

A multiplexer is worth understanding structurally, because it's a clean example of the layered model solving a real problem. When you run tmux on the remote server, tmux itself becomes a long-running process on that server. Your shells run inside tmux, not directly under your SSH session. Your SSH session attaches to tmux, and tmux relays input and output between your emulator and the shells it's hosting. When your SSH connection dies, tmux doesn't die — it's not attached to your fragile network connection, it's a normal server process — and the shells inside it don't die either, because their controlling terminal is the tmux session, not the SSH connection. You reconnect, run `tmux attach`, and your shells are right where you left them, output and all. The multiplexer also gives you multiple panes and windows inside a single SSH connection, which is useful, but the persistence is the part that earns the complexity.

The deeper lesson worth carrying out of this topic is that every "session" you've ever been in has been a chain of these attachments, and the chain is exactly as strong as its weakest link. Your emulator is attached to a shell. The shell is attached to a kernel via its controlling terminal. The processes the shell launched are attached to that same terminal by default. Locally, the whole chain lives on one machine and is robust; remotely, one link in the chain crosses a network, and networks fail. Multiplexers and `nohup` are not exotic tools — they're the things you reach for once you understand that a process's lifetime is bounded by what it's attached to, and you want to choose what it's attached to deliberately.

The skill this topic builds, then, isn't "knowing tmux commands." It's the ability to look at any remote-work situation and trace, layer by layer, what's running where, attached to what, and what would happen if any single link broke. Once you can do that, deciding whether a given job needs `nohup`, a multiplexer, a proper service manager, or nothing at all stops being a judgment call and starts being a derivation.

Worth a deeper pass because these are the features that turn SSH from "log into a server" into a general-purpose secure-tunneling tool, and most engineers use one or two of them without realizing the others exist.

#### L1-10 · Terminal Multiplexing, Remote Sessions, and SSH
Once you understand that the terminal emulator, shell, and OS are separable layers, remote work stops being mysterious: SSH connects your local terminal emulator to a *shell running on a different machine's kernel entirely*, with your local emulator doing nothing but display and input-forwarding the whole time. This is the topic that finally explains why closing your laptop can kill a remote session (the local emulator/connection died, taking the remote shell's controlling terminal with it) and why tools like `tmux`/`screen` or `nohup` exist specifically to detach a running process from that fragile connection.
